2.5.3 Examination Culture and Teacher-Centered Pedagogy as Systemic Barriers

Peer-reviewed studies of Libyan English-as-a-foreign-language (EFL) classrooms consistently document an examination-centered, teacher-led instructional culture emphasizing grammar explanation, translation, and memorization—often misaligned with policy ambitions for communicative competence and learner autonomy. In a seminal study of Libyan secondary schools, Orafi and Borg (2009) found that teachers’ actual classroom practices were dominated by grammar–translation and textbook preparation for high-stakes assessments, despite curricular intentions to implement communicative approaches. At the system level, official profiles similarly describe centralized, summative examinations as the principal mechanism of progression, reinforcing a culture where factual recall and form-focused tasks are privileged as “what counts” for success (UNESCO-IBE, 2011). 

These assessment structures shape motivation: when advancement hinges on high-stakes exams testing recall rather than deeper understanding or communicative performance, students rationally orient their effort toward exam preparation. The result is an institutional barrier (assessment regimes privileging memorization) that produces motivational barriers (lower perceived value of exploratory, autonomous learning). Evidence from Libyan contexts indicates that even when teachers voice support for innovation, classroom realities remain anchored in examination preparation and teacher-centered routines (Orafi & Borg, 2009; Elabbar, 2011). In such environments, learner autonomy is undercut by both institutional constraints and the perceived utility of exam-oriented strategies over inquiry-driven learning.

2.5.4 Recent Libyan Research Advances (2023–2024) and Comparative Insights

Emerging classroom work in Libya (2023–2024)—including tertiary-level interventions and teacher action research—indicates that Libyan students’ motivation is responsive to pedagogical innovation, particularly interactive speaking activities (e.g., structured role-play, simulation, and task-based dialogue) that connect English to authentic communicative purposes. While historical accounts emphasize persistent exam focus in Libyan classrooms (Orafi & Borg, 2009; Elabbar, 2011), recent Libyan classroom implementations report elevated willingness to participate, greater enjoyment, and increased self-reported motivation when students are offered engaging communicative tasks rather than exclusive focus on grammar-and-translation drills. These Libya-based findings align with broader MENA-region evidence showing significant motivational gains when teaching shifts toward meaningful interaction, collaborative tasks, and authentic language use.

From the perspective of Expectancy–Value Theory (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000), traditional memorization-heavy curricula tend to suppress intrinsic interest and provide low utility value beyond examinations. By contrast, innovative pedagogy increases both perceived utility (English as a tool for real communication) and intrinsic value (enjoyment and engagement), thereby strengthening autonomous motivation. The comparative insight is clear: Libyan students’ disposition is not immutably exam-focused; rather, motivation is a function of opportunity structures. When classrooms provide authentic, interactive experiences that make English useful and engaging, intrinsic motivation rises and exam-centric attitudes recede.
