Title: Students’ Attitude Toward Autonomous Learning in Higher Education in Libya: A Study of Engagement and Motivation

1. Introduction
Autonomous learning—students’ capacity, willingness, and actions to self-direct their learning—has become essential in higher education. Globally, theories such as Self-Determination Theory (SDT), social cognitive theory, and constructivism emphasize autonomy as foundational for motivation, deep engagement, and lifelong learning (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Bandura, 1997; Vygotsky, 1978; Biggs & Tang, 2011). In Libya, the drive to reform post-conflict higher education intersects with challenges in infrastructure, safety, and resource availability alongside commitments to modernize curricula and digital learning (UNESCO, 2022). Libyan EFL studies show teachers’ positivity towards learner autonomy and students’ readiness for autonomous practices—particularly via out-of-class and ICT-mediated learning—but also reveal barriers tied to exam-orientation, limited autonomous skills, rules favoring teacher-centered methods, and insufficient resources (Algwil, 2024; Saleh, 2016; Mustapha & Mahmoud, 2019; Altaleb, 2024). This proposal seeks to systematically investigate attitudes, engagement, motivation, and socio-pedagogical factors shaping autonomous learning in Libyan HE, generating evidence to inform policy and practice.

2. Literature Review (summary; see Chapter_2_Literature_Review.md for detailed review)
- Theoretical frameworks: SDT (autonomy, competence, relatedness), social cognitive (self-efficacy, modeling), constructivism (active, social learning), heutagogy (self-determined learning), connectivism (digital networks).
- Attitudes: Students and teachers in Libya show positivity toward autonomy, but misconceptions (e.g., learning without a teacher) are rejected; teacher facilitation is deemed essential (Saleh, 2016).
- Engagement: Multidimensional—behavioral, emotional, cognitive, and agentic engagement—enhanced by goal-setting, self-monitoring, autonomy-supportive feedback, and ICT tools (Fredricks et al., 2004; Reeve, 2013; Altaleb, 2024).
- Motivation: Autonomous motivation sustains persistence and deep learning; Libya’s exam-centric context emphasizes performance goals; interventions should pivot toward mastery goals (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Wigfield & Eccles, 2000).
- Barriers: Procrastination, time management, anxiety (Steel, 2007; Pekrun, 2006); teacher-centered traditions; resource constraints (Algwil, 2024; UNESCO, 2022).
- Strategies: Scaffolding, formative feedback, negotiated choice, social participation, ICT-mediated communities, learning analytics dashboards.

3. Research Gaps
- Limited post-2011 empirical studies on autonomous learning in Libyan HE, particularly integrating technology’s role amid infrastructural constraints.
- Underexplored cultural dimensions—exam orientation, assessment practices, norms about teacher authority—and their impact on autonomy, motivation, and engagement.
- Sparse research on gender differences, regional inequalities, and discipline-specific autonomy (STEM vs. humanities) in Libyan universities.
- Insufficient evaluation of ICT-mediated autonomous learning (e.g., social media, dashboards), especially in resource-variable environments.

4. Methodology
4.1 Design
- Mixed-methods, sequential explanatory design: Phase 1 survey (quantitative), Phase 2 interviews/focus groups (qualitative), Phase 3 classroom/online activity observations (optional).
- Focus on attitudes, engagement, motivation, and perceived barriers/facilitators to autonomy.

4.2 Sampling and Sites
- Universities: Misurata University, Zawia University, Alasmarya Islamic University (Zliten), Sabratha University (plus optional: University of Tripoli).
- Sampling: Stratified random sampling across faculties (STEM, humanities), academic year levels, and gender.
- Target N: Quantitative survey n ≈ 400; qualitative interviews n ≈ 40 (teachers and students), 6–8 focus groups.

4.3 Instruments
- Attitudes toward autonomous learning: Adapted items drawing on Fisher, King, & Tague’s SDL readiness components (DOI: 10.1054/nedt.2001.0589) and autonomy scales aligned with SDT (Niemiec & Ryan, 2009).
- Engagement: Student Engagement Instrument (SEI) cognitive/psychological scales (Appleton et al., 2006; DOI: 10.1016/j.jsp.2006.04.002).
- Motivation: SDT-based subscales capturing autonomous vs controlled regulation.
- Technology use: Frequency and depth of ICT/social media usage for learning; out-of-class activities; perceptions of utility and barriers.
- Demographics: University, faculty, level, gender, prior ICT experience.

4.4 Procedures
- Survey: Online (institutional emails/platforms) and paper distribution with standardized instructions; consent obtained.
- Interviews: Semi-structured, capturing experiences with autonomous learning, barriers, enabling factors, and perceived outcomes; tailored for post-conflict sensitivity (trauma-informed interviewing).
- Focus groups: Cross-faculty student groups discussing autonomy practices and experiences.
- Observations: Optional classroom and online activity logs (with consent) to document autonomy-supportive practices and student agentic engagement.

4.5 Ethical Considerations
- Ethics approval: Institutional review boards (IRBs) across participating universities.
- Adherence to BERA (2011) guidelines: informed consent, confidentiality, anonymity, voluntary participation, right to withdraw.
- Post-conflict sensitivity: avoid triggering content; ensure safe environments; provide contact information for support.
- Data protection: secure storage; de-identification; restricted access.

5. Data Analysis
5.1 Quantitative
- Descriptive statistics (attitudes, engagement, motivation scores; ICT usage).
- Reliability checks (Cronbach’s alpha).
- Correlational analyses (autonomy attitudes vs engagement/motivation).
- Regression modeling (predictors of engagement and motivation; examine gender, faculty, level effects).
- Optional: Factor analysis for measurement validation.

5.2 Qualitative
- Thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke framework), coding for autonomy facilitators, barriers (exam-orientation; resource constraints; teacher roles), ICT enablers, and cultural dynamics.
- Triangulation across interviews and focus groups; cross-site comparison (urban vs semi-urban; discipline differences).

6. Expected Contributions
6.1 Policy-Level
- Evidence for curriculum reforms embedding choice, self-monitoring, and formative assessment.
- Recommendations for integrating social media and ICT in structured autonomy pathways, mindful of resource constraints.
- Support for EMIS and learning analytics to foster self-awareness and agentic engagement.

6.2 Practice-Level (Teacher Empowerment)
- Faculty development in autonomy-supportive pedagogy (SDT-aligned), feedback practices, negotiated assessment.
- Toolkits for scaffolding autonomy: goal-setting templates, reflective logs, peer collaboration protocols, ICT checklists.

6.3 Student Outcomes
- Enhanced attitudes, engagement, and autonomous motivation.
- Improved academic performance and resilience; increased out-of-class learning.

7. Timeline and Feasibility
- Month 1–2: Ethics approvals; instrument adaptation/piloting.
- Month 3–4: Survey deployment; initial analysis.
- Month 5–6: Interviews/focus groups; qualitative analysis.
- Month 7: Integration and reporting; dissemination to stakeholders.

8. Limitations and Mitigation
- Connectivity variability: provide paper options; offline data collection; lightweight platforms.
- Institutional heterogeneity: stratified sampling and site-level adjustments.
- Cultural sensitivities: collaborative design with local educators; pilot testing.
