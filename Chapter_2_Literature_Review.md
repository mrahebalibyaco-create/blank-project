Chapter 2: Literature Review
Title: Students’ attitude toward autonomous learning in higher education: A study of engagement and motivation

Aims of the study
- To investigate higher education students’ attitudes toward autonomous learning.
- To understand how autonomy influences their engagement and motivation.
- To identify factors that encourage or inhibit students’ willingness to engage in autonomous learning.

Research questions
Q1. What are higher education students’ attitudes toward autonomous learning?
Q2. How does autonomous learning influence students’ engagement and motivation?
Q3. What factors encourage or inhibit students’ willingness to engage in autonomous learning?

2.0 Introduction: The Imperative for Autonomous Learning in the 21st Century
Autonomous learning has moved from a niche pedagogical ideal to an imperative competency underpinning success in the contemporary higher education landscape. The knowledge economy, rapid technological change, and workplace demands for adaptability intensify the need for graduates who self-direct their learning, critically evaluate information, and collaborate across diverse settings. The foundations of autonomous learning lie in humanistic traditions that value learner agency and self-direction (Dewey, 1916; Rogers, 1983) and in adult education concepts such as lifelong learning (Jarvis, 2007). In applied higher education contexts, learning autonomy now converges with constructivist pedagogy, self-determination theory (SDT), social cognitive theory, and modern theories such as connectivism and heutagogy. In Libya and related Arab-region contexts, efforts to reform education, expand ICT access, and reconfigure teacher roles underscore both the necessity and complexity of fostering autonomy. Post-conflict constraints—such as damaged infrastructure, safety risks, and resource shortages—coexist with aspirations to modernize curricula and improve student outcomes (UNESCO, 2022). As a result, this review integrates global theory with Libyan evidence to map attitudes, engagement, motivation, and barriers, and to offer policy and pedagogical implications suited to the local context.

2.1 Defining Autonomous Learning: From Concept to Praxis
2.1.1 Historical and Seminal Definitions
Knowles’s andragogy emphasized adult learners’ capacity for self-direction—planning, implementing, and evaluating their learning (Knowles, 1975). Boud (1981) argued autonomy is “a vital requisite” for functioning effectively in modern society, and Little (1991; 2004) framed autonomy as learning how to learn intentionally, involving planning, monitoring, and evaluating. Holec (1981) famously defined autonomy as “the ability to take charge of one’s own learning,” highlighting decision-making across goals, methods, resources, and evaluation.

2.1.2 A Contemporary Operational Definition
SDT positions autonomy as a fundamental psychological need, alongside competence and relatedness, which when satisfied fosters intrinsic motivation, internalization of values, and well-being (Ryan & Deci, 2000; DOI: 10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68). Operationally, autonomous learning entails the learner’s willingness and capacity to self-regulate—setting mastery-oriented goals, monitoring progress, employing strategies, and seeking feedback—within supportive environments that provide choice, optimal challenges, and meaningful rationales (Niemiec & Ryan, 2009; DOI: 10.1177/1477878509104318).

2.1.3 Distinguishing Autonomous Learning from Related Concepts
Autonomous learning overlaps with self-regulated learning (SRL) and independent learning but differs in locus and breadth. SRL focuses on metacognitive, motivational, and behavioral processes for specific tasks (Zimmerman, 2002; DOI: 10.1207/s15430421tip4102_2). Autonomy additionally emphasizes volition, internalization, and the learner’s role in shaping conditions of learning. Independent learning often implies learning without direct teacher presence; autonomy, by contrast, does not mean learning without a teacher, but learning with teachers who adopt facilitative roles. Libyan EFL university teachers explicitly reject the misconception that “Learner autonomy means learning without a teacher” and affirm that “Learner autonomy cannot develop without the help of the teacher” (Saleh, 2016, p. -211-; Zawia University Bulletin ISSUE No.18-Vol.(2)).

2.2 The Critical Role of Autonomous Learning in Higher Education
2.2.1 Alignment with 21st-Century Skills
Autonomy supports critical thinking, problem-solving, adaptability, and creativity—competencies repeatedly associated with academic and professional success (Dweck, 2006; Biggs & Tang, 2011). Autonomy promotes deeper mastery, transfer of knowledge, and resilience in complex tasks.

2.2.2 A Prerequisite for Lifelong Learning
Autonomous learners are more likely to continue learning, rethink assumptions, and update skills across their careers (Jarvis, 2007; DOI: 10.4324/9780203964408). In Libya’s evolving HE system, fostering autonomy is central to recovery, reform, and participation in the global knowledge economy.

2.2.3 Impact on Academic and Professional Success
Meta-analyses associate autonomous motivation and related constructs (academic self-efficacy, effort regulation) with higher GPA and persistence (Richardson, Abraham, & Bond, 2012; DOI: 10.1037/a0026838). Autonomy support from instructors predicts better engagement, conceptual learning, and long-term maintenance of behaviors (Niemiec & Ryan, 2009).

2.3 Objectives and Scope of This Review
2.3.1 To synthesize theoretical frameworks underpinning autonomous learning (SDT, social cognitive theory, constructivism, heutagogy, connectivism).
2.3.2 To critically analyze empirical research on attitudes, engagement, and motivation—including Libyan HE contexts and comparable Arab-region studies.
2.3.3 To identify practical implications, challenges, and research avenues tailored to post-conflict, resource-constrained, and culturally exam-oriented settings.

2.1 Theoretical Bedrock of Autonomous Learning
2.1.1 Self-Determination Theory (SDT): The Psychology of Autonomy
2.1.1.1 Core Principles
SDT posits three basic psychological needs—autonomy, competence, relatedness—whose satisfaction catalyzes intrinsic motivation, internalization, and well-being (Ryan & Deci, 2000; DOI above).

2.1.1.2 The Three Needs
Autonomy reflects volition and self-endorsement; competence involves effectance and mastery; relatedness entails belonging and care. Contexts that support choice, optimal challenges, and warm relationships promote autonomous forms of motivation; controlling contexts undermine them (Cognitive Evaluation Theory; organismic integration).

2.1.1.3 Empirical Applications in Higher Education
Autonomy-supportive teaching predicts greater curiosity, deeper processing, and agentic engagement (Reeve, 2013; DOI: 10.1037/a0032690). In Libyan contexts, teachers emphasize creating comfortable atmospheres and collaborative work as antecedents of independent learning (Algwil, 2024; interview data). This aligns with SDT’s emphasis on supportive climates for internalization and sustained engagement.

2.1.2 Social Cognitive Theory: Self-Belief and Observation
2.1.2.1 Self-Efficacy as Cornerstone
Bandura’s (1997) self-efficacy predicts willingness to undertake autonomous tasks and persist. Students’ belief in capability moderates the adoption of SDL strategies and authentic tasks.

2.1.2.2 Observational Learning and Modeling
Students learn autonomy strategies by observing peers and instructors who model goal-setting, self-monitoring, and strategic resource use (Schunk, 2012). Teacher modeling is pivotal in transitioning from teacher-centered to learner-centered pedagogy.

2.1.2.3 Triadic Reciprocal Causation
Personal factors, behavior, and environment interact (Zimmerman, 2002). In Libya, institutional policies, resource conditions, and teacher beliefs shape student autonomy—showing practical implications of reciprocal causation.

2.1.3 Constructivism: Learning as an Active Process
2.1.3.1 Knowledge Construction
Constructivist frameworks (Vygotsky, 1978; Biggs & Tang, 2011) view learning as socially mediated, active, and iterative. Autonomy is scaffolded through inquiry, collaboration, and authentic tasks.

2.1.3.2 Implications for Autonomy
Constructivist teaching fosters independence and discovery via problem-based learning (Hmelo-Silver, 2004; DOI: 10.1007/s10648-004-0005-3), formative feedback (Hattie & Timperley, 2007; DOI: 10.3102/003465430298487), and negotiated assessment.

2.1.4 Emerging and Complementary Theories
2.1.4.1 Heutagogy
Heutagogy extends andragogy, centering on self-determined learning where learners select pathways and engage in capability development for complex, adaptive contexts (Hase & Kenyon, 2007).

2.1.4.2 Connectivism
Connectivism frames learning in digital networks, emphasizing capacity to navigate, connect, and curate knowledge across platforms and communities (Siemens, 2005).

2.2 The Student Perspective: Deconstructing Attitudes Toward Autonomous Learning
2.2.1 The Nature of Attitude: Cognitive, Affective, Behavioral Components
Students’ beliefs (cognitive), feelings (affective), and intentions (conative) shape willingness to adopt autonomy. In Libyan HE, many instructors report students appreciate collaborative work and feedback, but exam orientation can dampen autonomous attitudes (Algwil, 2024).

2.2.2 Factors Shaping Student Attitudes
2.2.2.1 Individual Learner Characteristics
Personality traits (e.g., conscientiousness), prior experiences, and academic self-concept influence readiness for autonomy (McCrae & Costa, 1997; Marsh & O’Mara, 2008).

2.2.2.2 Sociocultural Influences
Collectivist traditions value shared responsibility; autonomy is often misunderstood as learning alone. Libyan teachers reject the notion that autonomy excludes teachers, underscoring cultural adaptation of autonomy (Saleh, 2016, p. -211-).

2.2.2.3 Educational Environment
Institutional culture, curriculum design, and instructor support are decisive. Zawia study findings show strong agreement that “Learner-centred classrooms provide ideal conditions for developing learner autonomy” (Saleh, 2016, p. -213-). Students’ positive attitudes towards ICT-mediated autonomy are also documented (Altaleb, 2024, p. 91).

2.2.3 Methodologies for Assessing Attitudes
2.2.3.1 Quantitative Measures
Readiness scales for autonomy (e.g., Fisher, King, & Tague, 2001; DOI: 10.1054/nedt.2001.0589) and student engagement instruments (Appleton et al., 2006; DOI: 10.1016/j.jsp.2006.04.002) provide metrics for cognitive/psychological engagement.

2.2.3.2 Qualitative Insights
Interviews and focus groups elucidate nuanced barriers and facilitators—e.g., Algwil’s interviews identify exam fixation: “They keep saying we know the evaluation will be mid-term 40 and final exam 60.” (Algwil, 2024, Omer interview).

2.3 The Dynamics of Engagement in Autonomous Learning Contexts
2.3.1 A Multidimensional Model of Engagement
Behavioral (time on task), emotional (interest, belonging), cognitive (deep processing), and agentic (proactive shaping of instruction) engagement are complementary (Fredricks et al., 2004; Reeve, 2013).

2.3.2 The Interplay Between Attitude, Motivation, and Engagement
Positive attitudes predict engagement; intrinsic motivation translates attitudes into sustained behaviors (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Libyan students’ preference for out-of-class learning and advanced-level goals indicates affective readiness and behavioral engagement (Mustapha & Mahmoud, 2019).

2.3.3 Evidence-Based Strategies to Foster Engagement
Goal-setting, self-monitoring, and authentic tasks enhance cognitive and agentic engagement (Zimmerman, 2002; Reeve, 2013). ICT platforms, simulations, and collaborative tools strengthen participation and autonomy (Wang et al., 2019, exemplified locally by Telegram group use in Algwil, 2024).

2.4 The Engine of Motivation: Fueling the Autonomous Learner
2.4.1 Motivational Spectrum: Intrinsic vs Extrinsic
SDT’s continuum spans external, introjected, identified, integrated regulation, and intrinsic motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Autonomous motivation—identified/integrated—yields better persistence and quality of learning.

2.4.1.2 The Power of Intrinsic Motivation
Intrinsic interest drives deep learning and vitality; autonomy-supportive contexts foster interest and reduce performance-only orientations.

2.4.2 Key Motivational Drivers
2.4.2.1 Goal Orientation
Mastery goals (understanding) outperform performance goals (grades) in sustainability and transfer (Elliot, 1999). Libya’s exam-centric norms emphasize performance goals; shifting to mastery is central to reform.

2.4.2.2 Task Value and Relevance
Perceived importance, utility, and interest correlate with motivation (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). Libyan students’ extensive ICT use and social media for academic goals signals perceived utility (Altaleb, 2024, pp. 99–102).

2.4.3 Tangible Impact of Motivation
2.4.3.1 Academic Performance
Autonomy readiness correlates positively with performance (Mustapha & Mahmoud, 2019).

2.4.3.2 Persistence and Resilience
Autonomous motivation enables retention and coping with challenges (Tinto, 1993). In resource-limited contexts, autonomous learners flexibly use social networks and online resources to sustain progress.

2.5 Navigating the Hurdles: Challenges and Barriers to Autonomous Learning
2.5.1 Student-Centered Barriers
Procrastination, time management deficits, and limited metacognitive strategies impede autonomy (Zimmerman, 2002; Steel, 2007; DOI: 10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65). Anxiety and fear of failure compound avoidance (Pekrun, 2006).

2.5.2 Institutional and Pedagogical Barriers
Lack of guidance, mentoring, and resources; teacher-centered traditions; high-stakes exams discourage autonomy (Kember, 2000; Boud, 1995). Libyan teachers report faculty rules and regulation constraints and insufficient resources (Algwil, 2024). Zawia study highlights tensions around assessment autonomy (Saleh, 2016, p. -213-).

2.5.3 Strategies for Overcoming Barriers
Scaffolding autonomy with gradual release of responsibility (Van de Pol et al., 2010), structured workshops on SRL, and faculty development for autonomy-supportive teaching. In Libyan HE, leveraging social media groups and negotiated timelines increased flexibility and ownership (Algwil, 2024).

2.6 Translating Theory into Practice: Implications for Stakeholders
2.6.1 Pedagogical Approaches
Student-centered learning environments (Weimer, 2013), inquiry-based and PBL enhance self-direction and mastery (Hmelo-Silver, 2004).

2.6.2 The Pivotal Role of the Educator
Teachers shift from lecturer to facilitator, modeling autonomous behaviors and providing formative feedback (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). Libyan teachers’ emphasis on safe, welcoming environments and collaboration illustrates autonomy-supportive practices (Algwil, 2024 interviews; Altaleb, 2024).

2.6.3 Institutional Policy and Support Structures
Design curricula with choice and flexibility; create ecosystems that include libraries, writing centers, ICT services, and learning analytics dashboards for self-awareness (Siemens & Baker, 2012; DOI: 10.1145/2330601.2330661). UNESCO’s national statement prioritizes digital learning, teacher development, and EMIS—aligning macro-level policy with autonomy needs (UNESCO, 2022).

2.7 Synthesis, Emerging Paradigms, and Avenues for Inquiry
2.7.1 Synthesis of Key Themes: An Integrated Model
SDT, social cognitive theory, and constructivism converge on the Attitude–Motivation–Engagement nexus: autonomy-supportive environments foster positive attitudes; internalized motivation drives agentic and sustained engagement; outcomes include performance, persistence, and well-being.

2.7.2 Emerging Paradigms
2.7.2.1 AI-Powered Learner
Adaptive systems personalize autonomy pathways (Brusilovsky, 2001; DOI above), with potential for Libya’s HE to deploy lightweight, offline-first solutions given connectivity constraints.

2.7.2.2 Learning Analytics for Self-Awareness
Dashboards enable self-monitoring and reflection, advancing agentic engagement and SRL (Siemens & Baker, 2012).

2.7.2.3 New Pedagogical Frontiers
SOLEs and open educational practices (Cronin, 2017; DOI: 10.1080/02680513.2017.1336143) extend learner agency beyond formal settings—particularly relevant where infrastructure limits traditional instruction.

2.7.3 Critical Avenues for Future Inquiry
Longitudinal studies on attitude development; mixed-methods in post-conflict contexts; cross-cultural analyses; discipline-specific autonomy interventions; the digital autonomy paradox—where technology both enables and constrains autonomy—requires empirical scrutiny in Libya’s variable infrastructure.

2.8 Conclusion
2.8.1 Recapitulation
Autonomous learning is essential for contemporary HE success. Theories (SDT, SCT, constructivism) converge on the need for autonomy-supportive climates that cultivate mastery goals, deep engagement, and resilient motivation. Libya-focused evidence reveals positive teacher and student attitudes toward autonomy, strong student preferences for out-of-class and ICT-enabled learning, and persistent barriers including exam orientation, resource constraints, and institutional rules.

2.8.2 Imperative for Action
Libyan universities should systematically embed autonomy in curricula, instruction, assessment, and student support—leveraging social media, ICT tools, and learning analytics while prioritizing teacher development and infrastructure reliability. Macro-level commitments (UNESCO, 2022) can be translated into actionable autonomy pathways to improve student outcomes.

2.8.3 Final Thought
Cultivating truly autonomous learners in Libya—supported by teachers as facilitators and institutions as enablers—has transformative potential for individuals, communities, and the nation’s rebuilding and innovation agendas. Autonomy is not optional; it is foundational to higher education’s mission in the 21st century.
