Libya_Autonomous_Learning_Research – Sources Log (2000–2025)

Purpose
- This folder logs sources, exact quotations, page references (where available), and URLs for the topic:
  Students’ Attitude Toward Autonomous Learning in Higher Education in Libya: Engagement and Motivation.

Libyan-priority sources (PDFs and journals)

1) Algwil, K. A. (2024). Libyan EFL Teachers’ Beliefs about Learner Autonomy at a Libyan University. Misurata University Journal (SATIL). 
   URL: https://journals.misuratau.edu.ly/satil/upload/file/R-2644-Libyan%20EFL%20Teachers%E2%80%99%20Beliefs%20about%20Learner%20Autonomy%20at%20a%20Libyan%20University.pdf
   Type: Mixed-methods (questionnaire + semi-structured interviews)
   Key quotes (interviews; page numbers not indicated in scraped text):
   - “When I ask my students to prepare presentation, some of them prepare slides and others are reluctant. They keep saying we know the evaluation will be mid-term 40 and final exam 60.” (Omer interview)
   - “I create a safe and welcoming environment… rapport of trust is established. This encourages students to work hard and to engage in the learning process efficiently.” (Sara interview)
   - “I establish shared group on the Telegram to exchange their thoughts and ideas and to share their knowledge… foster active learning and a shared sense of responsibility.” (Questionnaire finding; learning-as-social-participation)
   Obstacles highlighted: exam-orientation; students’ lack of autonomous skills; faculty rules favoring teacher-centered approaches; insufficient resources/ICT.

2) Mustapha, S. M., & Mahmoud, F. T. M. (2019). Autonomous Learning Readiness and English Language Performance of EFL Libyan Secondary School Students. 
   URL: https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2019/90603/90603.pdf
   Type: Mixed-methods; 103 students; SPSS + NVivo
   Key quote (no pages visible in scrape):
   - “The findings showed that the Libyan secondary school students were ready to carry out autonomous learning… Students preferred to learn English outside the classroom… [and] autonomous learning readiness was significantly correlated to English language performance.”

3) Saleh, S. E. (2016). Exploring EFL University Teachers’ Conceptions of Learner Autonomy. University Bulletin, Zawia University.
   URL: https://bulletin.zu.edu.ly/issue_n18_2/Contents/E_11.pdf
   Type: Semi-structured questionnaire; 48 Libyan EFL university teachers
   Page markers in PDF: -208-, -211-, -213- (University Bulletin ISSUE No.18-Vol.(2) May 2016)
   Key quotes:
   - “Learner autonomy means learning without a teacher.” (Item wording; rejected by participants) [p. -211-]
   - “Learner autonomy cannot develop without the help of the teacher.” (Item 18; agreed) [p. -211-]
   - “Learner-centred classrooms provide ideal conditions for developing learner autonomy.” (Item 28; agreed) [p. -213-]
   Obstacles noted: lack of facilities; student hesitation to share responsibility; accountability pressures from exams; imposed materials.

4) Altaleb, S. B. (2024). Integrating Technology and Social Media to Foster Language Learner Autonomy: Students’ Perception. Comprehensive Journal of Science, 9(33), 91–106.
   Article page: https://cjos.histr.edu.ly/index.php/journal/article/view/37
   PDF: https://cjos.histr.edu.ly/index.php/journal/article/view/37/29/64
   Key quotes (with page indications from journal pagination):
   - “The findings confirmed that Libyan university students place a high value on the significance of social media as a means to improve their English language skills and demonstrated a pronounced trend towards autonomous learning to learn English outside the classroom.” (Abstract, p. 91)
   - “I use ICT to prepare for presentations in the classroom and to look up references.” (Interviewee 5; Results section ~ pp. 99–102)
   Pedagogical implication: social media and ICT strengthen autonomous readiness and collaborative learning.

5) UNESCO (2022). Transforming Education – State of Libya: National Statement of Commitment (Transforming Education Summit).
   URL: https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/ed3002/Libya_National_Statement_of_Commitment.pdf
   Key quotes (no page markers provided in scrape):
   - “Between 2011 and 2019, at least 37 schools were destroyed and 182 were partially damaged… In 2020 alone, at least 16 schools had been hit by air strikes, affecting 15,890 students.”
   - Commitments include: improving national curriculum; teacher development; digital learning and transformations; EMIS strengthening.

6) World Bank (2007). ICT in Education in Libya (infoDev/World Bank).
